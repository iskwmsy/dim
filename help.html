Layer Duration

[ Trim the selected layer’s in point to the playhead
] Trim the selected layer’s out point to the playhead

The bar on an object’s own row in the timeline is its duration — the object is drawn only while the playhead is inside it. Drag either end to trim, and drag the bar to move the whole span in time (its keyframes stay put). Dragging snaps softly to the playhead, the timeline ends, the layer’s own first and last keyframe, and the edges of its other segments. Unlike the eye icon in the scene tree — which hides an object outright and is saved with the scene — the duration only affects the object over time, and both must allow it for the object to appear. A trimmed layer shows a bar with diagonal stripes when its eye is also off. The shortcuts above act on the segment under the playhead (a press in a gap does nothing) and apply to the whole selection.

Several appearances from one layer. Use the cut button on the layer’s own row (left of the ◇ keyframe button) to split its duration at the playhead, or ⌥ click a bar to split it at the point you click. Then drag one piece away to open a gap — the layer now appears twice on the timeline without duplicating it. Drag a segment into a neighbour and the two merge; segments that merely touch stay separate (they look continuous either way, since the object is visible across both). Trim a segment past its own opposite end to delete it — it turns red while the pointer is down to say so — and the last remaining segment clamps instead of vanishing. Double-click resets the layer to the full timeline. One thing to know: the segments share one animation. A layer’s keyframes keep running through the gaps, so a second appearance shows the object wherever its animation has reached by then — it is a second appearance, not a replay of the first.

Shoot on Ns

Click the line between two keyframes to open the curve editor; its Shoot on field holds each pose for N frames across that segment — 1 = every frame (“ones”), 2 = twos, 3 = threes — the hand-animated stepped look. Easing still shapes the motion: the held poses are samples of the eased curve, and the editor draws the staircase that actually plays over the dimmed curve so you can see it. Steps restart at the LEFT keyframe, so a key’s pose always appears exactly on its own frame.

It works on any track that has a curve editor — an object’s position or rotation, a 3D or fold parameter, and the Camera and Workplane rows too, so a stepped camera segment gives a stop-motion camera move. Because it lives on the segment, you can step one motion while the rest of the animation stays smooth, or step several tracks separately; tracks keyed at different frames step on their own timing rather than in lockstep. Set it back to 1 to go smooth again. The setting is saved with the animation and undone with the animation undo (⌥ X), like every other curve edit.

Looping

The same curve editor carries Loop After and Loop Before, which keep an animation going outside its keyframes — after the last one and before the first. Unlike everything else in that window, these apply to the whole attribute track, not just the segment you clicked: looping describes what happens beyond the ends of the animation, so it can only belong to the track as a whole. Open any segment of that track to see or change it. Each attribute loops on its own — X can cycle while Y stays put.

Looping repeats the animation from the first keyframe’s value. Looping with Offset continues from the last keyframe’s value instead, adding the whole change again each time — a step forward keyframed once becomes a walk that keeps travelling. Pingpong plays the animation forward, then backward, then forward again. On the Before side the same three run into the first keyframe, with Offset flowing continuously into it. Easing and Shoot on replay in every cycle, and it works on any track — including vertex (Shape) keys, so a two-pose morph can cycle forever, and the Camera, where an offset loop on Azimuth gives an endless turntable.

With plain Looping the value jumps back at the seam whenever the first and last keyframes differ — that is the nature of a cycle, so match the two poses when you want it seamless, or use Offset or Pingpong. Keyframing inside a looped stretch turns that moment into a real keyframe and the loop moves out beyond it. A looping track SHOWS its repeats: the region beyond the keys is drawn with faded copies of that track’s own keyframes on a faded line, so you can see the rhythm the loop will play (Pingpong mirrors them, and Looping with Offset repeats on the same beats because it shifts values rather than timing). They are just an indication — you cannot click or drag them. Loop settings are saved with the scene and undone with the animation undo (⌥ X). Note this is different from the Pingpong checkbox in the timeline bar, which bounces playback over the whole timeline rather than extending one track (that checkbox was labelled “B&F” before).

Hold Framing (the vertigo / dolly‑zoom shot)

The Camera Settings Keep Size checkbox holds the subject’s size while you edit Dist or FOV by hand. Its animated twin is the Hold Framing dropdown, the first row under Camera in the timeline. Set it to FOV follows Dist and keyframe Dist alone: the lens widens as the camera moves in, the subject stays exactly the same size, and the perspective distorts around it — the Hitchcock shot. Dist follows FOV is the same shot driven the other way: keyframe the lens and the camera moves itself to hold the framing.

The size being held is Framing — a track of its own, and shown live in the Lens section. Leave it unkeyed for a pure vertigo; keyframe it if you also want the subject to grow or shrink during the move.

Dist, FOV and Framing are three numbers tied by one relationship, so only two of them can ever be free — the dropdown chooses which one the other two calculate. That one greys out and loses its keyframe diamond, because it is now an output: under Free that is Framing, under FOV follows Dist it is FOV, under Dist follows FOV it is Dist. Keyframes already on a channel that becomes derived are kept, just ignored, and come back when you switch away.

Why not simply keyframe Dist and FOV together? Because they interpolate independently, so the framing is only correct at the keyframes — on a typical vertigo the subject swells about 40% in the middle of the move and shrinks back. Hold Framing computes the derived channel after interpolation instead, so the framing is exact on every frame, and easing, Shoot on and looping all still work on the channel you keyed. Perspective cameras only: in orthographic and oblique, distance does not change apparent size, so there is nothing to trade off and the control is disabled (your setting is remembered and resumes when you go back to perspective). If a framing is impossible at some distance the lens stops at 179° and the FOV row turns red for those frames.

Animating vertices (Shape keys)

A path’s shape can be keyframed: expand a path layer and use the Shape › Vertices row. Its keyframe diamond records the whole path’s current vertex positions as one pose, and the path morphs between poses — so an extruded, revolved, folded or surface-mapped object morphs with it, and a morphing path used as a sweep rail carries its surface along. With Auto-Key armed, editing vertices with the Direct Select (A) tool — dragging or arrow-nudging — records a pose automatically. Key Selected in the timeline’s top bar also records a pose while the A tool has vertices selected — it turns blue to say so; with no vertex selection it keyframes the object’s transform as before. Easing and Shoot on work per segment, exactly like any other track. Parametric shapes (spiral, star, text…) must be baked to a path first.

Editing the numbers. Expanding Shape lists one row per vertex for the vertices that are animated, plus any you have selected with the A tool (shown in the accent colour). Each row edits that vertex’s X / Y / Z together; a dot marks every keyframe where that vertex changes. Values are raw local coordinates — not the display units of the Transform panel. Editing a row while a shape keyframe is selected changes that pose; editing between keyframes creates a new pose at the playhead, keeping every other vertex exactly where it already was. There is no per-vertex timing: a pose keys as a whole, which is what keeps the shape consistent.

Editing the path afterwards. Most structural edits now carry the animation with them: adding a point (on Bézier and straight paths) re-splits every pose at the same place, so no pose changes shape at all; removing a point and reversing the direction apply the same change to every pose. Switching Path Type (Linear / B-spline / Bézier) keeps the animation too — it only changes how the same vertices are drawn, so the poses still apply and the curve morphs. Edits that cannot keep the poses ask first and remove the vertex animation only if you agree — cutting with the scissors, converting to Bézier, reordering anchors, and adding a point to a B-spline (where the new point becomes a control point). Joining paths is simply not offered while a path has vertex animation. Anything not covered still pauses the animation rather than corrupting it: the Vertices label turns red and the per-vertex rows hide until you undo the edit. Dropped animation can be brought back with the animation undo (⌥ X).

Auto-Key

With the Auto-Key checkbox armed (shown in red), every property you edit records a keyframe at the playhead — from any panel, tool drawer, arrow-key nudge, or canvas drag — creating the track on first touch. Exceptions: camera navigation (orbit / truck / wheel / HUD bars) only re-keys a camera that is already animated, so looking around never starts a camera animation; deliberate edits in the Camera panel do record. Value edits inside the timeline panel always create keyframes, armed or not — the timeline is the animation editor. “Key Selected” snapshots the full transform of the selection regardless of Auto-Key. Auto-Key always starts OFF.

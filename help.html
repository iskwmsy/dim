The tool panel carries the extrusion's Cap and Render Settings while the tool is active (a mirror: the same controls stay in 3D Settings). Center Origin moves the object’s own origin to the middle of its points, and Bake Pivot moves it to wherever the pivot is — nothing moves on the canvas, Position then reads that point and the pivot reads 0 (handy for a pen path, which is drawn with its origin at the world origin). The Transform panel’s Bake Pivot is the same command. Shape keys move with the points, and one Undo takes the whole step back. The origin stays where it is (hover the button for the reason) when something depends on it: an extrude or revolve taper, a revolve whose axis passes the origin (Center: Object), a Sweep profile, Spline Flow or mapping on the object, position or pivot keyframes, or a parametric shape (Bake to Path first). Bake Pivot then does what it did before: Position takes the pivot’s place, the origin stays.

Layer Duration

[ Trim the selected layer’s in point to the playhead
] Trim the selected layer’s out point to the playhead

The bar on an object’s own row in the timeline is its duration — the object is drawn only while the playhead is inside it. Drag either end to trim, and drag the bar to move the whole span in time (its keyframes stay put). Dragging snaps softly to the playhead, the timeline ends, the layer’s own first and last keyframe, and the edges of its other segments. Unlike the eye icon in the scene tree — which hides an object outright and is saved with the scene — the duration only affects the object over time, and both must allow it for the object to appear. A trimmed layer shows a bar with diagonal stripes when its eye is also off. The shortcuts above act on the segment under the playhead (a press in a gap does nothing) and apply to the whole selection.

Several appearances from one layer. Use the cut button on the layer’s own row (left of the ◇ keyframe button) to split its duration at the playhead, or ⌥ click a bar to split it at the point you click. Then drag one piece away to open a gap — the layer now appears twice on the timeline without duplicating it. Drag a segment into a neighbour and the two merge; segments that merely touch stay separate (they look continuous either way, since the object is visible across both). Trim a segment past its own opposite end to delete it — it turns red while the pointer is down to say so — and the last remaining segment clamps instead of vanishing. Double-click resets the layer to the full timeline. One thing to know: the segments share one animation. A layer’s keyframes keep running through the gaps, so a second appearance shows the object wherever its animation has reached by then — it is a second appearance, not a replay of the first.

Shoot on Ns

Click the line between two keyframes to open the curve editor; its Shoot on field holds each pose for N frames across that segment — 1 = every frame (“ones”), 2 = twos, 3 = threes — the hand-animated stepped look. Easing still shapes the motion: the held poses are samples of the eased curve, and the editor draws the staircase that actually plays over the dimmed curve so you can see it. Steps restart at the LEFT keyframe, so a key’s pose always appears exactly on its own frame.

It works on any track that has a curve editor — an object’s position or rotation, a 3D or fold parameter, and the Camera and Workplane rows too, so a stepped camera segment gives a stop-motion camera move. Because it lives on the segment, you can step one motion while the rest of the animation stays smooth, or step several tracks separately; tracks keyed at different frames step on their own timing rather than in lockstep. Set it back to 1 to go smooth again. The setting is saved with the animation and undone with the animation undo (⌥ X), like every other curve edit.

Looping

The same curve editor carries Loop After and Loop Before, which keep an animation going outside its keyframes — after the last one and before the first. Unlike everything else in that window, these apply to the whole attribute track, not just the segment you clicked: looping describes what happens beyond the ends of the animation, so it can only belong to the track as a whole. Open any segment of that track to see or change it. Each attribute loops on its own — X can cycle while Y stays put.

Looping repeats the animation from the first keyframe’s value. Looping with Offset continues from the last keyframe’s value instead, adding the whole change again each time — a step forward keyframed once becomes a walk that keeps travelling. Pingpong plays the animation forward, then backward, then forward again. On the Before side the same three run into the first keyframe, with Offset flowing continuously into it. Easing and Shoot on replay in every cycle, and it works on any track — including vertex (Shape) keys, so a two-pose morph can cycle forever, and the Camera, where an offset loop on Azimuth gives an endless turntable.

With plain Looping the value jumps back at the seam whenever the first and last keyframes differ — that is the nature of a cycle, so match the two poses when you want it seamless, or use Offset or Pingpong. Keyframing inside a looped stretch turns that moment into a real keyframe and the loop moves out beyond it. A looping track SHOWS its repeats: the region beyond the keys is drawn with faded copies of that track’s own keyframes on a faded line, so you can see the rhythm the loop will play (Pingpong mirrors them, and Looping with Offset repeats on the same beats because it shifts values rather than timing). They are just an indication — you cannot click or drag them. Loop settings are saved with the scene and undone with the animation undo (⌥ X). Note this is different from the Pingpong checkbox in the timeline bar, which bounces playback over the whole timeline rather than extending one track (that checkbox was labelled “B&F” before).

Hold Framing (the vertigo / dolly‑zoom shot)

The Camera Settings Keep Size checkbox holds the subject’s size while you edit Dist or FOV by hand. Its animated twin is the Hold Framing dropdown, the first row under Camera in the timeline. Set it to FOV follows Dist and keyframe Dist alone: the lens widens as the camera moves in, the subject stays exactly the same size, and the perspective distorts around it — the Hitchcock shot. Dist follows FOV is the same shot driven the other way: keyframe the lens and the camera moves itself to hold the framing.

The size being held is Framing — a track of its own, and shown live in the Lens section. Leave it unkeyed for a pure vertigo; keyframe it if you also want the subject to grow or shrink during the move.

Dist, FOV and Framing are three numbers tied by one relationship, so only two of them can ever be free — the dropdown chooses which one the other two calculate. That one greys out and loses its keyframe diamond, because it is now an output: under Free that is Framing, under FOV follows Dist it is FOV, under Dist follows FOV it is Dist. Keyframes already on a channel that becomes derived are kept, just ignored, and come back when you switch away.

Why not simply keyframe Dist and FOV together? Because they interpolate independently, so the framing is only correct at the keyframes — on a typical vertigo the subject swells about 40% in the middle of the move and shrinks back. Hold Framing computes the derived channel after interpolation instead, so the framing is exact on every frame, and easing, Shoot on and looping all still work on the channel you keyed. Perspective cameras only: in orthographic and oblique, distance does not change apparent size, so there is nothing to trade off and the control is disabled (your setting is remembered and resumes when you go back to perspective). If a framing is impossible at some distance the lens stops at 179° and the FOV row turns red for those frames.

Animating vertices (Shape keys)

A path’s shape can be keyframed: expand a path layer and use the Shape › Vertices row. Its keyframe diamond records the whole path’s current vertex positions as one pose, and the path morphs between poses — so an extruded, revolved, folded or surface-mapped object morphs with it, and a morphing path used as a sweep rail carries its surface along. With Auto-Key armed, editing vertices with the Direct Select (A) tool — dragging or arrow-nudging — records a pose automatically. Key Selected in the timeline’s top bar also records a pose while the A tool has vertices selected — it turns blue to say so; with no vertex selection it keyframes the object’s transform as before. Easing and Shoot on work per segment, exactly like any other track. Parametric shapes (spiral, star, text…) must be baked to a path first.

Editing the numbers. Expanding Shape lists one row per vertex for the vertices that are animated, plus any you have selected with the A tool (shown in the accent colour). Each row edits that vertex’s X / Y / Z together; a dot marks every keyframe where that vertex changes. Values are raw local coordinates — not the display units of the Transform panel. Editing a row while a shape keyframe is selected changes that pose; editing between keyframes creates a new pose at the playhead, keeping every other vertex exactly where it already was. There is no per-vertex timing: a pose keys as a whole, which is what keeps the shape consistent.

Editing the path afterwards. Most structural edits now carry the animation with them: adding a point (on Bézier and straight paths) re-splits every pose at the same place, so no pose changes shape at all; removing a point and reversing the direction apply the same change to every pose. Switching Path Type (Linear / B-Spline / Bézier) keeps the animation too — it only changes how the same vertices are drawn, so the poses still apply and the curve morphs. Edits that cannot keep the poses ask first and remove the vertex animation only if you agree — cutting with the scissors, converting to Bézier, reordering anchors, and adding a point to a B-Spline (where the new point becomes a control point). Joining paths is simply not offered while a path has vertex animation. Anything not covered still pauses the animation rather than corrupting it: the Vertices label turns red and the per-vertex rows hide until you undo the edit. Dropped animation can be brought back with the animation undo (⌥ X).

Auto-Key

With the Auto-Key checkbox armed (shown in red), every property you edit records a keyframe at the playhead — from any panel, tool drawer, arrow-key nudge, or canvas drag — creating the track on first touch. Exceptions: camera navigation (orbit / truck / wheel / HUD bars) only re-keys a camera that is already animated, so looking around never starts a camera animation; deliberate edits in the Camera panel do record. Value edits inside the timeline panel always create keyframes, armed or not — the timeline is the animation editor. Key Selected works regardless of Auto-Key (see below). Auto-Key always starts OFF.

Animated fields in the panels

A number field whose property has keyframes shows it in the accent colour: lighter while the playhead is between keys, full accent and bold when the playhead sits exactly on a key — the same rule the timeline’s diamonds follow. These fields also follow the playhead while you scrub or play. The tint means “the timeline lists this row and it has keys”, so a channel the timeline does not offer (a second 3D modifier, a derived Hold-Framing channel) never tints, and a greyed-out field never looks live. Covered: Transform, Style opacity and trim, the 3D modifier numbers, the Mapping tab (incl. per-crease fold rows), the Camera panel and the Workplane grid. Not covered: the shape drawers (spiral / text / …) and the HUD bars.

Starting an animation — Key Selected

Key Selected is how you start animating, and it listens rather than keying everything: channels that already have tracks get a keyframe at the playhead immediately, and channels without tracks are armed — the button gains an accent outline (“Listening”) and a hollow diamond marks the armed frame on the layer’s row. Nothing is keyed yet. Then scrub anywhere and edit: the first edit of an armed channel records two keyframes — the armed frame with the pose you armed, and the playhead with the new value — so the motion exists at once, and the channels you never touch never grow tracks. This works even with Auto-Key OFF: arming is the recording intent, scoped to the armed objects and one-shot per channel.

Press the button again while armed to cancel (and press once more to re-arm at the current frame — re-arming replaces the old pose). ⌥-click keys the full transform immediately, the old behavior — any armed channels commit their armed-frame key too. Undoing a recorded pair does not re-arm the pose (it was consumed); press Key Selected again. Armed poses are session-only: they are not saved with the scene and clear when a scene is loaded.

Remove static channels (the struck-through diamond next to the export buttons) deletes every track whose keyframes are all equal — including single-key tracks — without moving anything on screen: the constant simply becomes the object’s resting value. It acts on the selection when something is selected, otherwise on the whole timeline, and one animation undo (⌥ X) restores everything. Handy for cleaning scenes keyed with the old key-everything behavior.

Boolean groups

Always available: the Boolean button sits beside Group / Ungroup. To see a group's objects as they are, turn off that group’s own Boolean switch in 3D Settings (nothing is lost). A Boolean is a group with a switch on: it draws one result in the place of its objects, and the objects stay ordinary objects — select one (its row in the list, or Select Child on the canvas), move it, reshape it, keyframe it, and the result follows. Everything stays vector: the result is cut along exact curves and exports like any other art.

Boolean button / ⌘ B Select two or more objects: they become a group named Boolean_nnnn. The bottom object of the selection is the base, whatever the order you clicked in; the others start as Subtract. On a plain group the button switches that group on. One undo step.
The rows Each row of a Boolean group carries a small icon: its operation (Union / Subtract / Intersect / Drill — click it for the menu), the base mark on the bottom row, or a warning on an object the Boolean cannot use. Rows act from the bottom up: each row acts on everything below it. Move Up / Move Down and dragging change the order. A group among the rows is one operand.
Drill A fourth operation (the row's menu, or Operation in 3D Settings): the object cuts a hole and stays in it. The hole is the object grown by Clearance — a distance, the same gap all round, with sharp corners (a box's hole is a bigger box, a star's a bigger star, a cone's a cone set back along its axis), or with Round Corners on, an arc of the clearance's radius at every corner. So one object drills through a plate with clearance: move it and the hole moves with it. Any extruded outline (straight or curved, with holes) and any revolved profile can drill, and cones and cylinders; refused, with the reason in the menu: a sheet, an open surface, a group row, an extrusion along a leaning vector, a taper on anything but a cone, a revolve that is not a whole turn or is tapered, and an object scaled unevenly or sheared. The hole's walls follow the group's Cut Faces like any subtract (Base paints them in the base object's colours). Clearance is keyframable (the timeline row Boolean > Drill Clearance); at 0 the drill is a plain Union.
3D Settings, a group selected The Boolean switch (off = a plain group again; nothing is lost). Cut Faces: the colour of the faces a cutter leaves behind — the cutter’s own (a little darker), the base’s, or one colour. Seams: the lines where two objects meet — drawn like every other edge, with the base’s or the cutter’s stroke (Base Stroke / Cutter Stroke), with a colour and a width of their own (Seam Color, Seam Width as a multiple of the stroke), or not at all; Cut Color is the one colour of Custom cut faces. Two objects that lie side by side in one plane make one face there: drawn alike (the same fill and stroke), nothing is drawn between them; drawn otherwise, the line between them is a seam.
3D Settings, a row selected (Boolean Operand) Operation of that object. A selected row shows on the canvas as a dashed outline of the whole solid, so you can see how far a cutter reaches.
What can be used A closed path with 3D Extrusion or 3D Revolve on (live round corners included), or a closed flat path with neither — a sheet (below), or an open path with 3D Extrusion or 3D Revolve on — an open surface (below). An extrusion along a leaning vector (a slanted prism), a Cuboid (the 3D Shape tool's box, turned, scaled or sheared — no bake needed) and a revolve with a taper (Scale Start / Scale End: the profile grows or shrinks as it turns; through a whole turn its two ends meet at a step, which shows as a face) can be used too. Not: an open path with no depth, a path with no depth that is not flat, a path with open and closed parts, an open path with a taper, a leaning vector with a taper, a vector that lies in the path’s own plane, a Cuboid with a face edited by hand, Sweep, Loft, Fold, Spline Flow, Surface Mapping, 2D Mode, Face Camera, a taper to a point, a profile that crosses its revolve axis (or would, where its taper scales it). Such an object keeps its place in the group, is drawn as itself, and its row and the panel say what to change.
Sheets and open surfaces A flat path with no depth is a sheet; an open path extruded (a ribbon) or revolved (a lampshade) is an open surface. Both are faces with no thickness, the same from either side, and act alike. A sheet (or surface) minus an object has a hole where the object passes through it; intersected with an object it is a section of it; joined to an object, it passes into it. One that cuts (Subtract) removes nothing — it has no volume — and draws a score line where it crosses the surface: a seam, drawn as the Seams setting says (so None hides it). Intersect of two of them: an open surface stands for the volume it encloses — a revolved one its inside, closed to its axis; an extruded one the shape closed by the straight line between its ends — so a lampshade intersected with a sheet is the part of the sheet inside the shade. A curve that its closing line would cross (an S) encloses nothing, and the intersect is empty.
Open surfaces: two sides An open path with 3D Extrusion or 3D Revolve on (a ribbon, a lampshade) and its Side Color on shows its Fill on the outside and the Side Color on the inside (a palette runs along the inside) — in Clean, Simple and More Simple Sides, Mesh and Exact, and in a Boolean. The outside is the side away from what the surface encloses (a revolve closed to its axis, an extrusion closed by the line between its ends); a curve that encloses nothing (an S, a straight line) has its outside on the left of the path’s direction (Invert Winding swaps it). Silhouette paints one fill, the side colour. With Side Color off, both sides are the Fill, as before.
Caps and Side Color The objects keep their own look. A cap set to Side is painted in the side colour; a cap set to None is left open, and the inside of the result shows through the opening. A Side Color palette runs along the outline of the path, one colour a segment (Cycle or Interpolate), whatever the object’s Render mode says — a Boolean draws one form.
Among other objects A Boolean group always sorts and hides its own faces. To have it hide, and be hidden by, the rest of the scene, turn on Scene → Z-Sort with Masks, as for any other objects that pass through each other.
Known limits A heavy result (a vase with a window) costs about a tenth of a second a frame on one core while something moves. dim therefore works out a Boolean on several cores at once (background workers) and draws the same picture sooner — on a 10-core Mac a dragged or played frame is about 1.6–1.9× faster. To compare, open dim.html?booleanWorkers=0 (one core, as before).
Render → Exact The same engine for one object, no group needed: in 3D Settings set an extrusion’s or a revolve’s Render to Exact and it is drawn as the faces the eye sees, each cut exactly where the surface turns away — the outline is the true silhouette, with no mesh of strips, so a plain revolve is a path or two. Caps (Fill / Side / None) and a Side Color palette (one colour a segment) work as in a Boolean. An object the engine cannot take (a taper to a point, a leaning extrude vector with a taper, … — the list above) is drawn as Clean, and the line under Render says why. Files store Exact as Clean plus a marker, so an older dim opens them as Clean. While the object or the camera moves, Exact costs a few times what Clean does (a vase: about 50 ms a frame against 15).

3D Settings — Extrude, Revolve, Sweep, Loft

The 3D Settings tab gives a flat path a solid. One modifier is on at a time (an enabled Extrusion wins over Revolve, Revolve over Sweep, Sweep over Loft); each has its own Render Settings block (below). A group carries an Extrusion of its own that reaches every path inside it (Override Children makes the group's settings win over a child's). Every number here is keyframable; the modes and the colours are not.

3D Extrusion Depth along the Vector, in the path's own axes (the Extrude tool, U, drags the same vector). Symmetric grows it both ways from the path's plane. Cap Front / Back: Fill paints the cap in the object's fill, Side in the side colour, None leaves it open so the inside shows. Front is the cap on the + side of the extrusion's axis (for a Z extrusion, the side the Front Camera sees), Back the − side, whatever way the vector points. Taper scales the profile at each cap (1 = the full profile; Link Taper keeps the two together). A taper of exactly 0 cannot be used by the Boolean; the Cone tool stores a point as 0.001.
3D Revolve The profile turned about the Axis (X, Y or Z of the path's own space) through Center: the object's Pivot (move the pivot with J to move the axis), the object's own origin, or the World origin. Offset pushes the profile away from the axis; Start is where the turn begins and Angle its span, so End = Start + Angle (a fan from 30° to 120° is Start 30, Angle 90) and an Angle below 360 leaves a wedge open; Segs is how many steps the turn is drawn in. Taper Start / End scales the profile as it turns, so a whole turn meets itself at a step. Cap Start / End work as the extrusion's (Fill / Side / None) and close the open wedge. A profile that lies wholly on the far side of its axis is fine; one that crosses the axis still draws but is refused by the Boolean and by Exact.
Sweep (Profile × Rail) The path is carried along another path, the Rail Path (any path in the scene). Taper scales the profile from the rail's start to its end; Cap Start / End as above; Segments is how many stations along the rail the surface is built from. The profile keeps its orientation along the rail by a rotation-minimising frame, so it does not spin on a curve.
Loft (Profile Blend) The surface between this path and the Target Path, pushed Depth apart, in Segments steps. Interpolation Linear goes straight, Smooth eases the blend. Auto Align matches the two paths' starting points so the surface does not twist; untick it to keep the authored start. Cap Start / End as above. A loft has no Clean mode: Mesh, Layer or Stacks.
Side Color (a revolve's Ribbon Color, a sweep's or loft's Surface Color) The colour of the sides, with a Palette: Add Color gives the sides several colours, one per authored segment of the profile (Cycle) or blended across them (Interpolate). In Layer, Mesh and Stacks the palette runs along the depth instead, one colour per layer. Caps are never palette-coloured: Fill or the first side colour. With no side colour set and no stroke, a new extrusion shades its sides from the fill by itself.
2D Mode Draws the object flat on the screen at its projected position, ignoring the camera's depth: a label that is always readable. Everything below it in the group stands down too.
Face Camera X and Y turn the object about that axis to face the camera (a billboard); Plane turns it wholly. In a Camera Window a billboard still faces the main camera.
Bake to Faces At the bottom of an extrusion's Render Settings: replaces the extrusion by a group of plain faces as the current render mode draws them (Clean: the two caps, every straight side as a plane, every curved side as a band that keeps rendering as a curved face; Mesh / Layer / Stacks: their own cells, layers or slabs). Every face edits on its own. The Shape tool's shapes bake the same way.

Render modes (the Render Settings block)

Clean The signature look: the profile's own curves, each side one face, split where the surface turns away from the camera so the parts sort right (Silhouette Split, on by default; off keeps a side one piece). Sides: Full draws every side; Simple merges sides that face the same way; More Simple merges a whole run between silhouettes into one band. Streamline Strokes (More Simple) strokes only the silhouette, the rims and the sharp creases, not the band boundaries; Shoulder Circles keeps the circles at the top and bottom of a revolved form. A revolve's Divisions row in Clean sets the profile steps.
Mesh A grid of cells, Divisions U (along the depth or the rail) × V (around the profile). Curved Surface draws each cell with its two curved profile edges (a curved face) instead of four straight corners. Draw Points marks the grid's points (size, colour, circle or square).
Layer N copies of the profile along the depth or around the axis (Layers, 2 to 1024), each a closed compound path filled even-odd; the palette runs through them. A cap set to Side repeats its neighbour's colour so the stack keeps its full depth.
Stacks The cut the other way: N stations (Stacks) along the axis, each the solid's exact cross-section there, the two ends included (a vase becomes stacked rings, a donut two rings a station). For an extrusion or a sweep Axis (Y or X) and Angle turn the cutting direction in the profile's plane. Snap to Anchors puts a station at each of the profile's anchor heights instead of spacing them evenly (the Count greys).
Silhouette (revolve) One fill for the whole form, cut at its outline; Crease Lines adds a stroke where the profile has a sharp corner. Side colours are a single colour here; Add Color is not available.
Exact (extrude, revolve) The Boolean engine drawing one object: the faces the eye sees, cut exactly at the outline, few paths and no mesh. What it cannot take is drawn as Clean and the line under Render says why (see Boolean groups above).
Draw Points Each modifier has its own Draw Points row for the points of its surface; the Style tab's Draw Points marks a flat path only.

3D Mapping — Spline Flow, Surface Mapping, Motion Path

The 3D Mapping tab bends the art onto something else. Every mapping draws the original art, re-shaped each frame; nothing is baked. Spline Flow, Surface Mapping and Fold are exclusive with the 3D modifiers (an enabled modifier wins); Motion Path is a constraint on the position and works beside anything.

Spline Flow The Bend Along checkbox turns it on: the art bends along the Target Path. Offset slides it along the path as a fraction of the path's length (0 to 1, not a distance; H is the offset across it), so one keyframed Offset runs text along a curve at a constant speed. Overflow says what happens past the ends: None, Clip (cut off), Clip & Warp (the piece that leaves one end comes in at the other), Overflow (continues straight along the end tangent), Around (wraps round a closed path). Repeat lays copies along the path with a Gap, or Fit spreads the copies evenly over the whole length. Match Target (on) gives the art the target path's own position, rotation and scale; off, it bends from the target's origin only. Warp: Bank leans the art into the curve, Twist turns it about the path. Scale U / V and Rotation X / Y / Z are applied before the bend.
Surface Mapping Lays the art on the Target Surface, a path with an extrusion, revolve, sweep or loft on, like a decal. Mode: Decal keeps the art's own size; Fit (Longer), Fit U, Fit V scale it to the surface along that axis; Stretch fills the surface both ways. Axis U / V choose which of the art's axes run along and around the surface (a sign flips them). Scale, Offset and Overflow (per axis, the Spline Flow choices) and Rotation place it; Normal Offset lifts it off the surface along the surface's normal, so a decal on a cylinder can float a little above it.
Algorithm and Resolution Both mappings draw their pieces with Smooth (Curve Fit), fitted curves from the art's own segments, or Subdivide, straight pieces at the Resolution. Resolution matters in Subdivide only; in Smooth the outline is re-fitted from the source and the setting changes nothing. Spline Flow starts in Smooth, Surface Mapping in Subdivide, which is why the two can look different at the same settings. Silhouette Split cuts the pieces where the surface turns away so they sort right.
Motion Path The object's anchor rides the Target Path at Percent of its length; keyframe Percent to move it (0 to 300 laps a closed path three times under Around). Position: On Path puts the object exactly on the path (its own Position greys); On Path + Offset adds the object's Position as an offset; Keep Position moves it relative to where it sits. Overflow: Clip stops at the ends, Overflow continues straight past them, Around loops. Align to Path turns the object to follow the path's direction with the chosen axis forward; its own rotation then twists about the path. The pivot chooses which point of the object rides the path. Works on groups too (a text split to letters following a curve).
Backside (Style tab) What a mapped or folded sheet shows from behind. None: the same picture; Sheet: the parts folded past the sheet's plane reverse their layer order, like a folded sticker showing its backing; View: whichever side faces away from the camera reverses, and the mapping's sticker sections sort as one.

Fold / Wrap / Curl / Wave

An origami mapping for a flat path, or a whole group folded as one sheet (its paths are the layers printed on it). Pieces are true paper regions, each one path with its fill and stroke, so the result pastes into Illustrator as plain art. Mode picks the engine; Axis U / V set the sheet's own axes (U is the direction the creases are measured along, V across; the fold normal is U × V); Scale U / V stretches the sheet about its centre before folding.

Fold A list of straight creases, Add Crease adds one after the last. Each crease has Position U and Position V (where its line passes, as fractions of the sheet), Rotation (0 = across U), Angle (a positive angle folds up; through 180 and beyond is allowed), Fold Side (which side moves, Right +U or Left −U) and Extent A / Extent B, the part of its line that actually folds (0 to 1 of the line; paper outside the span stays put, which is what lets one sheet fold into a box). Auto Extent computes the extents from the other creases so flaps do not cross (the fields grey). The list order is the fold order: crease 1 folds first and carries the later creases' paper with it; Move Up and Remove reorder. A crease bends the paper and cuts only where a piece would otherwise hide itself; Force Split cuts at every crease (the look of the old renderer). On the canvas: drag the bar on a crease's guide to move it, the ticks at its ends to set the extents.
Wrap The flap bends round a cylinder of Radius starting at the crease (a sticker peeling off); past the bend the paper continues flat. Several creases make a chain of bends along one direction; their curvatures add where they overlap.
Curl The paper rolls into a spiral from the crease on: Direction Front / Back picks the side, Radius the starting radius, Pitch how much the radius grows per turn, Tighten winds the inner turns closer. Keyframe Position U to roll a map open or shut; a fully wound tube rolls away along the sheet.
Wave Zigzags and bumps as a crease generator: Bumps (one bump = a peak and a trough), Size (paper per flank), Angle (the sharpness; the Height / Width read-out follows), Shape Up / Down / Wave (symmetric about the sheet), Extend Forward / Backward / Both from the start line, Radius 0 for sharp zigzags or above 0 for rounded ones. Paper: Conserve keeps the paper's length, so a travelling wave pulls the sheet along like an inchworm; Stretch keeps the sheet in place and stretches the flanks, for a sheet that waves where it stands. Animate Angle at a fixed Size for an accordion that compresses as it sharpens.
Draw Crease A stroke on the crease lines themselves (width, colour, Ends, Dash), on bent creases only (a cut crease already shows its piece edge). Angle Fade brings it in from invisible at 0° to full at the set angle, so an animated fold develops its crease as it sharpens; Visible at 0° keeps the line on an unfolded sheet as a guide.
What it cannot do A crease crossing a region already bent by an earlier crease has no straight hinge there, so the far part separates: use Extent to stop the crease short. Wrap chains are parallel; a curl is always the last crease. Fold is exclusive with Extrusion / Revolve / Sweep / Loft; ungrouping a folded group discards the fold (it asks first).

Style tab — fill, stroke and the stroke features

Path Type / Convert to Bezier Path Type says how the same vertices are read: Linear, B-Spline (Cubic / Quadratic) or Cubic Bezier; the points are reinterpreted, never rebuilt. Convert to Bezier writes the drawn curve as explicit Bezier handles, so it stops reinterpreting (it asks first when the path has vertex animation).
Fill Colour and Opacity; Rule says how overlapping subpaths fill (Non-Zero counts the winding, Even-Odd alternates; Invert Winding in the Transform tab reverses the vertex order, which also flips which side is the front); Close Path closes the outline; Blend is the blend mode over what lies behind, exported as mix-blend-mode.
Stroke Colour, width (in the display unit), Opacity, Ends (the join: Miter / Round / Bevel, with M. Clip and Arcs as the SVG 2 variants Illustrator may not read; the cap: Butt / Round / Square) and Dash (dash gap dash gap, in stroke units; empty is solid).
Trim % Start and End, the part of the stroke that is drawn (0 to 100, like After Effects' Trim Paths; the fill stays whole). Trim Mode: Individual trims each subpath by the same share of its own length, Sequential runs one span through the subpaths in order (a write-on). Both ends are keyframable. The cut stays on the curve as the camera moves; a 3D modifier's body is not trimmed.
3D Stroke Off keeps the stroke's width flat on the screen. Perspective scales it with distance (Depth Strength: how strongly). Depth Range maps it over a near / far band (Width % Near and Far). Self-Occlude hides the parts of the stroke that lie behind the object itself. A 3D stroke is drawn, and exported, as an outlined filled shape.
Draw Points See Workplane & Other: the anchors of a flat path as dots (size, colour, circle or square).
Backside See 3D Mapping: what a mapped or folded sheet shows from behind.
A group: Z-Sort Children Orders the group's members by depth every frame, by Depth its direct children (each one block) or all descendants flattened into one order. Render Intersections adds the vector z-buffer: where members pass through each other, the hidden parts are cut away with masks, so the group exports as the picture you see. The Scene row does the same for the whole scene.

Scene Objects and the Transform tab

The list Every object in drawing order, groups folding open with their triangle. Each row has an eye (hide; saved with the scene) and a lock (no selection or move on the canvas). Drag rows to reorder or into a group; Group, Ungroup and Boolean sit above the list. A child selected inside a folded group is marked on its group's row. A 3D Shape is one row until it is baked.
Position / Rotation / Scale / Shear In the display unit (Rotation in degrees). Shear X is how far X moves per unit of Y (left field) and of Z (right), Shear Y and Shear Z likewise: six components, in local units, with the scale applied after the shear. Keyframable. The Shear tool (H) drags the same numbers.
Pivot / Piv Rot The pivot, in the object's own space, and the pivot rotation, the frame a scale or shear is applied in (the Pivot tool, J, edits both on the canvas). Reset Pivot puts both back to zero without moving the object; Bake Pivot moves the object's origin to the pivot (see J above).
Bake Scale / Shear Folds the scale and the shear into the vertices: the object looks the same with scale 1 and shear 0. With Scale Strokes (Settings) the Scale tool also bakes the stroke width and dash into the new size.
Flatten Path To Workplane projects the path onto the workplane along its normal, From Camera along the camera's view rays (what you see stays, the depth goes). Under the Direct Select tool the same buttons flatten only the selected vertices.
Adjust Pivot: Align to Workplane Moves the pivot onto the active workplane; the object stays where it is.
Vertex (Direct Select) Index: the selected vertex's number, with the previous / next arrows and Del; its position fields below. Show Vertex Index in the tool drawer numbers the anchors on the canvas.
Subpaths Count is how many subpaths the compound path holds. Merge Subpath joins the selected paths into one compound path (each becomes a subpath, for holes and counters); Purge Subpath splits a compound path into one path per subpath.

Shape tools (the drawers)

Cameras The list holds the scene's cameras (the presets Front / Top / Side / Isometric / Dimetric / Trimetric / Default and your own): Activate looks through the selected one, Load copies it into the active camera, the lock freezes it, Restore Preset Cameras brings back any missing preset and resets changed ones (Camera 1 and your own are left alone). Drag Control What a plain drag on the background does: Use Shortcut (the modifier keys above decide), or always Orbit, Distance, FOV or Truck & Pedestal.

The Camera Window's bars are the same set without the typing.

Camera Window

Opening it Camera Settings › Camera Window: a floating window that shows the scene from another camera while you work in the main view. Drag its header to move it, its bottom corners to resize. Auto Reload / Grid / HUD / Axis / Guide Auto Reload (on) refreshes the window when the scene changes, pacing itself on heavy scenes; off, it holds its picture and the reload button refreshes it by hand (its own camera edits always show). Paste from Illustrator

⌘ V with SVG on the clipboard (or the Paste SVG button with a file) imports the artwork onto the workplane, centred, as one object or one group per <g>. What imports:

Exactlypaths, rects (incl. rounded), circles, ellipses, lines, polygons, polylines; groups, layers, <switch> (Illustrator’s “Preserve Editing” export), <a>, nested <svg>; transforms; fill and stroke colours, opacity (layer opacity reaches its children), fill rule, stroke width, caps, joins, dashes; clipping masks (you choose Clip or Ignore; Ignore keeps the mask outline as a dashed path)
Approximately<use> / symbols (instanced as plain groups; a symbol’s own viewBox is not applied); gradients (flat fill of the first stop colour); patterns (mid grey); a clip drawn with curves (clipped along its control polygon)
Not at alltext (outline it in Illustrator first), images, masks (<mask>), filters, blend modes other than the element’s own

A paste that contains nothing drawable tells you so instead of silently doing nothing.

Saving & Autosave

⌘ S / Save Scene Writes the open scene to this browser's saved slot (localStorage) and says "Scene saved". A save that fails (storage quota) says so instead of failing silently. The slot is per browser profile, not per file — two tabs with different scenes share it.
Autosave Settings panel: Autosave checkbox + Every (min). Off by default. When on (every 2 minutes unless you change it) the open scene is written to the same slot on that interval whenever it has changed since the last save; it waits while playback, a cache capture, an animation export, an import or a drag is in progress. Success is silent; a failure shows a notice once and pauses autosave until the next successful Save.
Reload Saved Replaces the open scene with the saved slot (the last Save or autosave). Asks first. It is one undo step: Undo brings the open scene back. With no saved slot it loads the test scene.
Export / Import Scene The .json file. Export carries the reference image at any size; the saved slot caps it at ~2.5 MB and tells you once when it had to leave it out. A file that is not a dim scene, or one that fails to load, is refused with a notice and the open scene stays exactly as it was. An import, Test Scene, Clear Scene and Reload Saved each replace the whole scene as ONE undo step: ⌘Z brings the previous scene back, ⌥Z its cameras, ⌥X its animation.

Exports

Copy to Clipboard, Export SVG and both animation exports ship the artwork only: the background rectangle (filled with the frame colour when one is set, never stroked), the scene’s paths with their hidden parts expressed as nested clipping masks, and the Frame Guide when one is chosen. Axes, gizmos, selection highlights and reference images never export, whatever Tab is showing; the grid exports only when “Keep grid when hiding (Tab) & export” is on in the Workplane panel. Two exports of an untouched scene are byte-identical, so a file diff means a real change. The multi-layer animation export writes one frame_NNNN layer per frame, each self-contained (its clipping masks are renamed per frame).

The setting is remembered.
Move on the workplane A free drag — an object’s body, a vertex under the A tool, the move gizmo’s centre circle — normally moves on the camera plane. Two switches put it on the plane parallel to the workplane through the point where the drag started: set the gizmo to Workplane (W), or check Restrict to Workplane in the Select tool’s Move Delta section (it wins whatever the gizmo mode). The move follows the cursor exactly — the point you grabbed stays under the pointer as the plane recedes in perspective — and the workplane’s grid snap applies on that plane. When you look at the workplane almost edge-on the plane cannot be hit cleanly, so that drag falls back to the camera plane and the drag readout says so. Axis-locked drags (Shift, Z, D) and the gizmo handles are unaffected.
Camera Window Camera Settings › Camera Window: a floating window showing the scene from another camera (pick it in the window’s dropdown; drag the picture to orbit, wheel to zoom, the bars on it work like the canvas ones). Stereo (on the window’s third bar) shows the scene from a left and a right eye side by side so you can see it in depth by fusing the two: Separation is the eyes’ distance apart in scene units (0.6 to start; more is deeper, 0 is flat), and the select says how you will look: Cross (the left eye’s picture on the right — cross your eyes until the two become three and look at the middle one), Parallel (left on the left — look through the screen as if at something far away; this one only fuses while the two pictures are nearer than your own eyes, about a 400 px window), or Both (three pictures: the first two for parallel viewing, the last two for cross). With a perspective camera the two eyes look the same way and the camera’s target sits at the screen plane, nearer things in front of it and farther things behind; with an orthographic or oblique camera the eyes turn toward the target instead. These settings belong to the window for this session only — nothing is written to the scene or the main export. The two icon buttons in the window’s header export THIS window’s view the way Cmd+C and Export SVG export the main one: Copy puts it on the clipboard as SVG (in Stereo, one SVG with the pictures side by side in the order shown), Export saves it as a file (in Stereo, two files, one per eye, named _L and _R; the browser may ask once to allow the second download). Each eye’s picture arrives as a clip group cut to its own frame, so with a perspective camera nothing of the shifted picture spills past the paper or into the picture beside it.
Continuous Drag Settings › Continuous Drag (on by default): while you drag — a number field, an object or gizmo on the canvas, a vertex, a camera bar, the fold guide, a corner widget, the Camera Subwindow — the pointer is captured the moment it nears the edge of the screen, so the drag never stops there: a drawn cursor wraps around instead and the value keeps changing. Drags that stay away from the edge are untouched, and a plain click never captures. The browser shows a one-time notice the first time a drag reaches an edge; Esc releases the capture and ends that drag where it is (it does not deselect).
Drag Readout Settings › Drag Readout (on by default): while you move an object or a vertex the canvas draws the vector from where the drag started to where it is now, with dashed legs for its world x, y and z, each carrying a small grey pill — the same pill as the ones at the paper’s corner — with the amount moved along it and its letter just above the pill (x over 71.13, z over −153.46 — a minus when that coordinate went down), and the total distance (Δ) on a dashed dimension line beside the vector, every pill starts at a HOME spot out beyond the gizmo and travels in a straight line onto its own leg (or, for the distance, onto its dimension line) in proportion to how long that line has grown — held at home for the first few pixels, on the line from about 200 px, a leader joining the two in between; nothing jumps and nothing animates by itself. Which pills you get depends on the control you grab, and it is the same set at a click, during the drag and afterwards: an axis handle gives that axis (its world x / y / z components under a Local or Camera gizmo), a plane handle its two, the centre circle or a drag on the object itself all three, plus Δ whenever there is a distance. CLICK a handle without dragging and its pills appear at their homes at zero, ready to be typed into — a move with no drag; a leg’s pill has a home that is fixed for the whole gesture; the Δ pill (and the single pill of a one-axis move) sits square to its own dimension line — on the perpendicular through the line’s middle, on the side away from the leg pills (the right, for a vertical one-axis move) — so a pill moves only because its line grew, shrank or turned; a pill that has reached its line is centred on it, and no line is drawn behind a pill; a click on the Camera gizmo’s Z handle or on a rotate ring does the same for the depth and the angle. A pill you SCRUB travels with its value; a pill you TYPE into stays put until you finish; a ring marks where the drag started and a filled dot where the object is now; while you drag a gizmo control the rest of the gizmo is hidden — only the axis, plane or centre circle in use stays (for an axis under the Select and Direct Select tools just its lettered square: the readout’s own line shows the axis), and a drag on the object itself keeps the centre circle — and after you let go the gizmo keeps that focus while the move is still pending — the control you used at full strength, the rest faint but still grabbable (they light up under the pointer, so a second drag on another axis needs no extra step) — the x, y and z of the move stand in a column at the paper’s top-left, each a pill you can click to type into or drag to scrub, and the distance rides its own dimension line on the canvas (a single-axis move puts that axis’s pill there instead), like the rotate tool’s angle pill; below the column two pills do what they say: Enter submits the transform and brings the whole gizmo back (starting another drag on a faint handle confirms it too, so each drag is its own undo step), ⌘Z / Ctrl+Z reverts it; when the move runs along a single world axis there is one dimension line, lettered with that axis; a label that has no room on its leg steps aside and points back to it, and the labels keep clear of the gizmo; and the x / y / z components and the distance as a vertical list of pills at the paper’s top-left corner (x, y, z, Δ, each labelled to the right of its pill). The pills are the Move Delta form’s fields on the canvas: they appear as soon as an object (or a vertex under the A tool) is selected, at zero, and stay while it is selected. DRAG a pill left or right to scrub its value (Shift ten times faster, Option ten times finer), or CLICK it to type: either applies as you go, from where the last drag started; Enter commits the move, Esc puts the previous values back. The Δ pill scrubs the distance along the current direction, so it does nothing until a drag or a scrub has given the move a direction. The picture on the canvas appears once a drag has moved a few pixels, and it STAYS after you let go — the start ring, the vector, the legs and their numbers — until the next operation (a press on the canvas, another selection or tool, undo / redo, another timeline frame), following any value you type or scrub in the pills; the plane or axis guide belongs to the drag itself and goes when you let go; and, on the guide itself — above the plane patch, or at the far end of the axis line, where it stays put while the gizmo moves — the plane or axis the drag is on (Camera plane; Screen X / Screen Y for a Shift lock and Camera Z axis for the Z key — those locks follow the camera, not the world; X axis or Local X axis for an axis handle; XZ plane; Workplane when the gizmo is set to Workplane or Restrict to Workplane is on; Camera plane (workplane edge-on) when that plane could not be used; Depth for the D key; 2D frame; “snapped” when Snap to Point has taken over). The plane or axis is also drawn under the vector: a translucent patch of the plane, or a dashed line along the axis, through the point where the drag started. When the drag ends, the Select tool’s Move Delta form holds that drag’s delta, so you can refine a rough drag by typing exact values (they apply from where the drag started) or press Cancel to put the selection back where the drag began. A move straight along the line of sight — the Camera gizmo’s Z handle, or a drag with D held — cannot be drawn where it happens (it points at your eye and the object does not shift on screen), so it shows ONE pill beside the gizmo: the amount, plus toward the camera, a minus away, titled z. It stands still for the whole drag; type another axis afterwards and the ordinary picture returns. The ROTATE tool has the same helper: while you turn a ring, a dashed line marks the ring’s zero direction (the next gizmo axis round: y for the X ring, z for Y, x for Z; three o’clock for the outer ring), a solid line marks where it has turned to, and the arc between them — just outside the ring, with a light sector — shows the turn, while a pill parked beside the ring’s axis label (it stays put while the angle runs) reads the angle of THIS drag to one decimal (a minus for a negative turn; past a full turn the pill keeps counting and a faint full circle appears). It is the drag’s true angle about the ring, which the drawer’s Euler Delta fields cannot show. Both stay after you let go, until the next operation. After you let go, the x / y / z and Δ pills AT THE DRAG work like the ones at the paper’s corner: CLICK one to type a value, DRAG it to scrub — sideways (right is more) or up and down (UP is more); whichever way you start is the way that scrub goes (Shift ten times faster, Option ten times finer), Tab / Shift+Tab to walk x → y → z → Δ, Enter to commit the move, Esc to put the previous values back. The pill you are editing stays a pill — it lights up with an accent ring and you type straight into it — and stays where it is while the picture moves around it, its line following it, and touching a pill does not dismiss the picture (a press anywhere else on the canvas still does). While a drag is in flight the pills are not clickable. The gizmo is monochrome and lights the control you hover or drag in the accent color; Settings › Colored Gizmo Highlight (off by default) brings back a hue per axis (red / green / blue, and yellow / cyan / magenta for the planes). Nothing of the picture shows through a pill: the vector, the dashed lines and the dots are cut where a pill covers them. The older x / y / z / Δ pills at the paper’s top-left corner are gone (and their Settings switch with them); everything they did is done by the pills at the gizmo — to type a move with no drag first, click a gizmo control and type into the pill that appears. The single depth pill (z) and the rotate tool’s angle pill are editable the same way: type or scrub the distance toward the camera (the object stays exactly on its line of sight), or the angle about the ring you turned — any angle, past a full turn or negative; the rotate pill scrubs at the ring’s own half a degree per pixel, Enter commits through the Rotate drawer’s Submit and Esc puts the orientation back exactly. The readout is on the canvas only: it never reaches the export or the Camera Subwindow.
Custom Color Picker Settings › Custom Color Picker (on by default; untick it to get the browser’s own colour dialog back). Clicking any colour swatch — fill, stroke, side colours and their palette rows, points, the paper, the grid, the accent — opens dim’s own picker beside the panel instead of the browser’s dialog: a saturation / brightness square, a hue bar, a dropdown that switches the three number fields between HSB, HSL and RGB (your choice is remembered), and a hex field that also takes the three-digit shorthand. The number fields are the app’s own: chevrons to step, and the drag handle on their left edge to scrub a value (Shift ×10, Alt ×0.1). Enter commits a typed value, ↑ / ↓ step it by 1 (10 with Shift). Below them: Shades — two tints and five shades of the colour you picked; opened from a side colour swatch (extrude Side Color, revolve ribbon, sweep / loft colour, a palette row) on an object that has a fill, the row reads Fill shades and offers shades of that fill, so a side that belongs to its face is one click — Harmony (the complement, the two neighbouring hues, the triad), Scene (the colours this scene actually paints — fills, strokes, side colours that are switched on, drawn points, the paper — most used first, read once each time the picker opens), and Recent (the last eight colours you ended a session on). All three are rows of round swatches, eight to a line. The selected shade is marked by a thin white square just inside it. A row never rearranges itself under the click that used it. The pipette beside the before / now chip is the eyedropper. In Chrome and Edge it picks a colour from anywhere on the screen. Safari and Firefox have no such browser feature, so there it picks from inside dim: click an object on the canvas to take its paint — the stroke if you click the stroke, otherwise the fill — or the paper, or any colour swatch in the panels and in the picker itself; a small round chip beside the pointer previews what you would get. It reads the object’s own colour, not the pixel, so opacity and blend modes are not mixed in; reference images and the gizmo are skipped. Esc cancels the pick either way. The chip at the top right shows the colour before and now; click its left half to go back. Esc or a click outside closes it; clicking another swatch moves it there. It changes only the swatch it was opened from, and an undo closes it. Off = the browser’s own picker, exactly as before.

Timeline (cursor over the panel)

Space Play / Stop
← / → Step current frame ±1
⌥ + ← / → Move selected keyframe(s) ±1 frame
Trash (Clear) Deletes the selected keyframe(s); with none selected, the focused track's keyframes; with no focus, the entire animation (asks first). A track that goes away takes its Loop Before / After with it and puts the object back at its rest value — including the pivot's hidden shift; Clear all also resets Hold Framing. All three are undoable with ⌥ Z.

The two numbers beside the frame counter read current / start–end and set the play range: playback, Go to Start, both SVG exports and the animation render cache all run from start to end, and frames outside it are dimmed on the ruler. Both edges are draggable handles on the ruler — the start handle appears once a start is set, and dragging it back to 0 parks it again (the number field brings it back). The start does not limit scrubbing — you can still drag the playhead anywhere on the timeline.

Layer Duration

[ Trim the selected layer’s in point to the playhead
] Trim the selected layer’s out point to the playhead

The bar on an object’s own row in the timeline is its duration — the object is drawn only while the playhead is inside it. Drag either end to trim, and drag the bar to move the whole span in time (its keyframes stay put). Dragging snaps softly to the playhead, the timeline ends, the layer’s own first and last keyframe, and the edges of its other segments. Unlike the eye icon in the scene tree — which hides an object outright and is saved with the scene — the duration only affects the object over time, and both must allow it for the object to appear. A trimmed layer shows a bar with diagonal stripes when its eye is also off. The shortcuts above act on the segment under the playhead (a press in a gap does nothing) and apply to the whole selection.

Several appearances from one layer. Use the cut button on the layer’s own row (left of the ◇ keyframe button) to split its duration at the playhead, or ⌥ click a bar to split it at the point you click. Then drag one piece away to open a gap — the layer now appears twice on the timeline without duplicating it. Drag a segment into a neighbour and the two merge; segments that merely touch stay separate (they look continuous either way, since the object is visible across both). Trim a segment past its own opposite end to delete it — it turns red while the pointer is down to say so — and the last remaining segment clamps instead of vanishing. Double-click resets the layer to the full timeline. One thing to know: the segments share one animation. A layer’s keyframes keep running through the gaps, so a second appearance shows the object wherever its animation has reached by then — it is a second appearance, not a replay of the first.

Shoot on Ns

Click the line between two keyframes to open the curve editor; its Shoot on field holds each pose for N frames across that segment — 1 = every frame (“ones”), 2 = twos, 3 = threes — the hand-animated stepped look. Easing still shapes the motion: the held poses are samples of the eased curve, and the editor draws the staircase that actually plays over the dimmed curve so you can see it. Steps restart at the LEFT keyframe, so a key’s pose always appears exactly on its own frame.

It works on any track that has a curve editor — an object’s position or rotation, a 3D or fold parameter, and the Camera and Workplane rows too, so a stepped camera segment gives a stop-motion camera move. Because it lives on the segment, you can step one motion while the rest of the animation stays smooth, or step several tracks separately; tracks keyed at different frames step on their own timing rather than in lockstep. Set it back to 1 to go smooth again. The setting is saved with the animation and undone with the animation undo (⌥ X), like every other curve edit.

Looping

The same curve editor carries Loop After and Loop Before, which keep an animation going outside its keyframes — after the last one and before the first. Unlike everything else in that window, these apply to the whole attribute track, not just the segment you clicked: looping describes what happens beyond the ends of the animation, so it can only belong to the track as a whole. Open any segment of that track to see or change it. Each attribute loops on its own — X can cycle while Y stays put.

Looping repeats the animation from the first keyframe’s value. Looping with Offset continues from the last keyframe’s value instead, adding the whole change again each time — a step forward keyframed once becomes a walk that keeps travelling. Pingpong plays the animation forward, then backward, then forward again. On the Before side the same three run into the first keyframe, with Offset flowing continuously into it. Easing and Shoot on replay in every cycle, and it works on any track — including vertex (Shape) keys, so a two-pose morph can cycle forever, and the Camera, where an offset loop on Azimuth gives an endless turntable.

With plain Looping the value jumps back at the seam whenever the first and last keyframes differ — that is the nature of a cycle, so match the two poses when you want it seamless, or use Offset or Pingpong. Keyframing inside a looped stretch turns that moment into a real keyframe and the loop moves out beyond it. A looping track SHOWS its repeats: the region beyond the keys is drawn with faded copies of that track’s own keyframes on a faded line, so you can see the rhythm the loop will play (Pingpong mirrors them, and Looping with Offset repeats on the same beats because it shifts values rather than timing). They are just an indication — you cannot click or drag them. Loop settings are saved with the scene and undone with the animation undo (⌥ X). Note this is different from the Pingpong checkbox in the timeline bar, which bounces playback over the whole timeline rather than extending one track (that checkbox was labelled “B&F” before).

Hold Framing (the vertigo / dolly‑zoom shot)

The Camera Settings Keep Size checkbox holds the subject’s size while you edit Dist or FOV by hand. Its animated twin is the Hold Framing dropdown, the first row under Camera in the timeline. Set it to FOV follows Dist and keyframe Dist alone: the lens widens as the camera moves in, the subject stays exactly the same size, and the perspective distorts around it — the Hitchcock shot. Dist follows FOV is the same shot driven the other way: keyframe the lens and the camera moves itself to hold the framing.

The size being held is Framing — a track of its own, and shown live in the Lens section. Leave it unkeyed for a pure vertigo; keyframe it if you also want the subject to grow or shrink during the move.

Dist, FOV and Framing are three numbers tied by one relationship, so only two of them can ever be free — the dropdown chooses which one the other two calculate. That one greys out and loses its keyframe diamond, because it is now an output: under Free that is Framing, under FOV follows Dist it is FOV, under Dist follows FOV it is Dist. Keyframes already on a channel that becomes derived are kept, just ignored, and come back when you switch away.

Why not simply keyframe Dist and FOV together? Because they interpolate independently, so the framing is only correct at the keyframes — on a typical vertigo the subject swells about 40% in the middle of the move and shrinks back. Hold Framing computes the derived channel after interpolation instead, so the framing is exact on every frame, and easing, Shoot on and looping all still work on the channel you keyed. Perspective cameras only: in orthographic and oblique, distance does not change apparent size, so there is nothing to trade off and the control is disabled (your setting is remembered and resumes when you go back to perspective). If a framing is impossible at some distance the lens stops at 179° and the FOV row turns red for those frames.

Animating vertices (Shape keys)

A path’s shape can be keyframed: expand a path layer and use the Shape › Vertices row. Its keyframe diamond records the whole path’s current vertex positions as one pose, and the path morphs between poses — so an extruded, revolved, folded or surface-mapped object morphs with it, and a morphing path used as a sweep rail carries its surface along. With Auto-Key armed, editing vertices with the Direct Select (A) tool — dragging or arrow-nudging — records a pose automatically. Key Selected in the timeline’s top bar also records a pose while the A tool has vertices selected — it turns blue to say so; with no vertex selection it keyframes the object’s transform as before. Easing and Shoot on work per segment, exactly like any other track. Parametric shapes (spiral, star, text…) must be baked to a path first.

Editing the numbers. Expanding Shape lists one row per vertex for the vertices that are animated, plus any you have selected with the A tool (shown in the accent colour). Each row edits that vertex’s X / Y / Z together; a dot marks every keyframe where that vertex changes. Values are raw local coordinates — not the display units of the Transform panel. Editing a row while a shape keyframe is selected changes that pose; editing between keyframes creates a new pose at the playhead, keeping every other vertex exactly where it already was. There is no per-vertex timing: a pose keys as a whole, which is what keeps the shape consistent.

Editing the path afterwards. Most structural edits now carry the animation with them: adding a point (on Bézier and straight paths) re-splits every pose at the same place, so no pose changes shape at all; removing a point and reversing the direction apply the same change to every pose. Switching Path Type (Linear / B-spline / Bézier) keeps the animation too — it only changes how the same vertices are drawn, so the poses still apply and the curve morphs. Edits that cannot keep the poses ask first and remove the vertex animation only if you agree — cutting with the scissors, converting to Bézier, reordering anchors, and adding a point to a B-spline (where the new point becomes a control point). Joining paths is simply not offered while a path has vertex animation. Anything not covered still pauses the animation rather than corrupting it: the Vertices label turns red and the per-vertex rows hide until you undo the edit. Dropped animation can be brought back with the animation undo (⌥ X).

Auto-Key

With the Auto-Key checkbox armed (shown in red), every property you edit records a keyframe at the playhead — from any panel, tool drawer, arrow-key nudge, or canvas drag — creating the track on first touch. Exceptions: camera navigation (orbit / truck / wheel / HUD bars) only re-keys a camera that is already animated, so looking around never starts a camera animation; deliberate edits in the Camera panel do record. Value edits inside the timeline panel always create keyframes, armed or not — the timeline is the animation editor. Key Selected works regardless of Auto-Key (see below). Auto-Key always starts OFF.

Animated fields in the panels

A number field whose property has keyframes shows it in the accent colour: lighter while the playhead is between keys, full accent and bold when the playhead sits exactly on a key — the same rule the timeline’s diamonds follow. These fields also follow the playhead while you scrub or play. The tint means “the timeline lists this row and it has keys”, so a channel the timeline does not offer (a second 3D modifier, a derived Hold-Framing channel) never tints, and a greyed-out field never looks live. Covered: Transform, Style opacity and trim, the 3D modifier numbers, the Mapping tab (incl. per-crease fold rows), the Camera panel and the Workplane grid. Not covered: the shape drawers (spiral / text / …) and the HUD bars.

Starting an animation — Key Selected

Key Selected is how you start animating, and it listens rather than keying everything: channels that already have tracks get a keyframe at the playhead immediately, and channels without tracks are armed — the button gains an accent outline (“Listening”) and a hollow diamond marks the armed frame on the layer’s row. Nothing is keyed yet. Then scrub anywhere and edit: the first edit of an armed channel records two keyframes — the armed frame with the pose you armed, and the playhead with the new value — so the motion exists at once, and the channels you never touch never grow tracks. This works even with Auto-Key OFF: arming is the recording intent, scoped to the armed objects and one-shot per channel.

Press the button again while armed to cancel (and press once more to re-arm at the current frame — re-arming replaces the old pose). ⌥-click keys the full transform immediately, the old behavior — any armed channels commit their armed-frame key too. Undoing a recorded pair does not re-arm the pose (it was consumed); press Key Selected again. Armed poses are session-only: they are not saved with the scene and clear when a scene is loaded.

Remove static channels (the struck-through diamond next to the export buttons) deletes every track whose keyframes are all equal — including single-key tracks — without moving anything on screen: the constant simply becomes the object’s resting value. It acts on the selection when something is selected, otherwise on the whole timeline, and one animation undo (⌥ X) restores everything. Handy for cleaning scenes keyed with the old key-everything behavior.

Paste from Illustrator

⌘ V with SVG on the clipboard (or the Paste SVG button with a file) imports the artwork onto the workplane, centred, as one object or one group per <g>. What imports:

Exactlypaths, rects (incl. rounded), circles, ellipses, lines, polygons, polylines; groups, layers, <switch> (Illustrator’s “Preserve Editing” export), <a>, nested <svg>; transforms; fill and stroke colours, opacity (layer opacity reaches its children), fill rule, stroke width, caps, joins, dashes; clipping masks (you choose Clip or Ignore; Ignore keeps the mask outline as a dashed path)
Approximately<use> / symbols (instanced as plain groups; a symbol’s own viewBox is not applied); gradients (flat fill of the first stop colour); patterns (mid grey); a clip drawn with curves (clipped along its control polygon)
Not at alltext (outline it in Illustrator first), images, masks (<mask>), filters, blend modes other than the element’s own

A paste that contains nothing drawable tells you so instead of silently doing nothing.

Saving & Autosave

⌘ S / Save Scene Writes the open scene to this browser's saved slot (localStorage) and says "Scene saved". A save that fails (storage quota) says so instead of failing silently. The slot is per browser profile, not per file — two tabs with different scenes share it.
Autosave Settings panel: Autosave checkbox + Every (min). Off by default. When on (every 2 minutes unless you change it) the open scene is written to the same slot on that interval whenever it has changed since the last save; it waits while playback, a cache capture, an animation export, an import or a drag is in progress. Success is silent; a failure shows a notice once and pauses autosave until the next successful Save.
Reload Saved Replaces the open scene with the saved slot (the last Save or autosave). Asks first. It is one undo step: Undo brings the open scene back. With no saved slot it loads the test scene.
Export / Import Scene The .json file. Export carries the reference image at any size; the saved slot caps it at ~2.5 MB and tells you once when it had to leave it out. A file that is not a dim scene, or one that fails to load, is refused with a notice and the open scene stays exactly as it was. An import, Test Scene, Clear Scene and Reload Saved each replace the whole scene as ONE undo step: ⌘Z brings the previous scene back, ⌥Z its cameras, ⌥X its animation.

Exports

Copy to Clipboard, Export SVG and both animation exports ship the artwork only: the background rectangle (filled with the frame colour when one is set, never stroked), the scene’s paths with their hidden parts expressed as nested clipping masks, and the Frame Guide when one is chosen. Axes, gizmos, selection highlights and reference images never export, whatever Tab is showing; the grid exports only when “Keep grid when hiding (Tab) & export” is on in the Workplane panel. Two exports of an untouched scene are byte-identical, so a file diff means a real change. The multi-layer animation export writes one frame_NNNN layer per frame, each self-contained (its clipping masks are renamed per frame).

Scene row The first row of the Scene Objects list is the scene itself. Omit hidden faces in export Data panel › Omit hidden faces in export (off by default): the SVG export and the clipboard copy leave out every object that contributes nothing to the picture — the back cap and back sides of an extrusion, faces of objects entirely behind others — so Illustrator gets a smaller file with no invisible objects. The faces that go were also filling the anti-aliasing gap between neighbouring faces, so an unstroked extrusion can show faint hairlines along its facet seams on screen (the vector art is exact; a stroke on the faces seals them). The setting is remembered.
Move on the workplane A free drag — an object’s body, a vertex under the A tool, the move gizmo’s centre circle — normally moves on the camera plane. Two switches put it on the plane parallel to the workplane through the point where the drag started: set the gizmo to Workplane (W), or check Restrict to Workplane in the Select tool’s Move Delta section (it wins whatever the gizmo mode). The move follows the cursor exactly — the point you grabbed stays under the pointer as the plane recedes in perspective — and the workplane’s grid snap applies on that plane. When you look at the workplane almost edge-on the plane cannot be hit cleanly, so that drag falls back to the camera plane and the drag readout says so. Axis-locked drags (Shift, Z, D) and the gizmo handles are unaffected.
Continuous drag Settings › Continuous drag (on by default): while you drag — a number field, an object or gizmo on the canvas, a vertex, a camera bar, the fold guide, a corner widget, the Camera Subwindow — the pointer is captured the moment it nears the edge of the screen, so the drag never stops there: a drawn cursor wraps around instead and the value keeps changing. Drags that stay away from the edge are untouched, and a plain click never captures. The browser shows a one-time notice the first time a drag reaches an edge; Esc releases the capture and ends that drag where it is (it does not deselect).
Drag readout Settings › Drag readout (on by default): while you move an object or a vertex the canvas draws the vector from where the drag started to where it is now, with dashed legs for its world x, y and z, each carrying a small grey pill — the same pill as the ones at the paper’s corner — with the amount moved along it and its letter just above the pill (x over 71.13, z over −153.46 — a minus when that coordinate went down), and the total distance (Δ) on a dashed dimension line beside the vector, every pill starts at a HOME spot out beyond the gizmo and travels in a straight line onto its own leg (or, for the distance, onto its dimension line) in proportion to how long that line has grown — held at home for the first few pixels, on the line from about 200 px, a leader joining the two in between; nothing jumps and nothing animates by itself. Which pills you get depends on the control you grab, and it is the same set at a click, during the drag and afterwards: an axis handle gives that axis (its world x / y / z components under a Local or Camera gizmo), a plane handle its two, the centre circle or a drag on the object itself all three, plus Δ whenever there is a distance. CLICK a handle without dragging and its pills appear at their homes at zero, ready to be typed into — a move with no drag; a leg’s pill has a home that is fixed for the whole gesture; the Δ pill (and the single pill of a one-axis move) sits square to its own dimension line — on the perpendicular through the line’s middle, on the side away from the leg pills (the right, for a vertical one-axis move) — so a pill moves only because its line grew, shrank or turned; a pill that has reached its line is centred on it, and no line is drawn behind a pill; a click on the Camera gizmo’s Z handle or on a rotate ring does the same for the depth and the angle. A pill you SCRUB travels with its value; a pill you TYPE into stays put until you finish; a ring marks where the drag started and a filled dot where the object is now; while you drag a gizmo control the rest of the gizmo is hidden — only the axis, plane or centre circle in use stays (for an axis under the Select and Direct Select tools just its lettered square: the readout’s own line shows the axis), and a drag on the object itself keeps the centre circle — and after you let go the gizmo keeps that focus while the move is still pending — the control you used at full strength, the rest faint but still grabbable (they light up under the pointer, so a second drag on another axis needs no extra step) — the x, y and z of the move stand in a column at the paper’s top-left, each a pill you can click to type into or drag to scrub, and the distance rides its own dimension line on the canvas (a single-axis move puts that axis’s pill there instead), like the rotate tool’s angle pill; below the column two pills do what they say: Enter submits the transform and brings the whole gizmo back (starting another drag on a faint handle confirms it too, so each drag is its own undo step), ⌘Z / Ctrl+Z reverts it; when the move runs along a single world axis there is one dimension line, lettered with that axis; a label that has no room on its leg steps aside and points back to it, and the labels keep clear of the gizmo; and the x / y / z components and the distance as a vertical list of pills at the paper’s top-left corner (x, y, z, Δ, each labelled to the right of its pill). The pills are the Move Delta form’s fields on the canvas: they appear as soon as an object (or a vertex under the A tool) is selected, at zero, and stay while it is selected. DRAG a pill left or right to scrub its value (Shift ten times faster, Option ten times finer), or CLICK it to type: either applies as you go, from where the last drag started; Enter commits the move, Esc puts the previous values back. The Δ pill scrubs the distance along the current direction, so it does nothing until a drag or a scrub has given the move a direction. The picture on the canvas appears once a drag has moved a few pixels, and it STAYS after you let go — the start ring, the vector, the legs and their numbers — until the next operation (a press on the canvas, another selection or tool, undo / redo, another timeline frame), following any value you type or scrub in the pills; the plane or axis guide belongs to the drag itself and goes when you let go; and, on the guide itself — above the plane patch, or at the far end of the axis line, where it stays put while the gizmo moves — the plane or axis the drag is on (Camera plane; Screen X / Screen Y for a Shift lock and Camera Z axis for the Z key — those locks follow the camera, not the world; X axis or Local X axis for an axis handle; XZ plane; Workplane when the gizmo is set to Workplane or Restrict to Workplane is on; Camera plane (workplane edge-on) when that plane could not be used; Depth for the D key; 2D frame; “snapped” when Snap to Point has taken over). The plane or axis is also drawn under the vector: a translucent patch of the plane, or a dashed line along the axis, through the point where the drag started. When the drag ends, the Select tool’s Move Delta form holds that drag’s delta, so you can refine a rough drag by typing exact values (they apply from where the drag started) or press Cancel to put the selection back where the drag began. A move straight along the line of sight — the Camera gizmo’s Z handle, or a drag with D held — cannot be drawn where it happens (it points at your eye and the object does not shift on screen), so it shows ONE pill beside the gizmo: the amount, plus toward the camera, a minus away, titled z. It stands still for the whole drag; type another axis afterwards and the ordinary picture returns. The ROTATE tool has the same helper: while you turn a ring, a dashed line marks the ring’s zero direction (the next gizmo axis round: y for the X ring, z for Y, x for Z; three o’clock for the outer ring), a solid line marks where it has turned to, and the arc between them — just outside the ring, with a light sector — shows the turn, while a pill parked beside the ring’s axis label (it stays put while the angle runs) reads the angle of THIS drag to one decimal (a minus for a negative turn; past a full turn the pill keeps counting and a faint full circle appears). It is the drag’s true angle about the ring, which the drawer’s Euler Delta fields cannot show. Both stay after you let go, until the next operation. After you let go, the x / y / z and Δ pills AT THE DRAG work like the ones at the paper’s corner: CLICK one to type a value, DRAG it to scrub — sideways (right is more) or up and down (UP is more); whichever way you start is the way that scrub goes (Shift ten times faster, Option ten times finer), Tab / Shift+Tab to walk x → y → z → Δ, Enter to commit the move, Esc to put the previous values back. The pill you are editing stays a pill — it lights up with an accent ring and you type straight into it — and stays where it is while the picture moves around it, its line following it, and touching a pill does not dismiss the picture (a press anywhere else on the canvas still does). While a drag is in flight the pills are not clickable. The gizmo is monochrome and lights the control you hover or drag in the accent color; Settings › Colored gizmo highlight (off by default) brings back a hue per axis (red / green / blue, and yellow / cyan / magenta for the planes). Nothing of the picture shows through a pill: the vector, the dashed lines and the dots are cut where a pill covers them. The older x / y / z / Δ pills at the paper’s top-left corner are gone (and their Settings switch with them); everything they did is done by the pills at the gizmo — to type a move with no drag first, click a gizmo control and type into the pill that appears. The single depth pill (z) and the rotate tool’s angle pill are editable the same way: type or scrub the distance toward the camera (the object stays exactly on its line of sight), or the angle about the ring you turned — any angle, past a full turn or negative; the rotate pill scrubs at the ring’s own half a degree per pixel, Enter commits through the Rotate drawer’s Submit and Esc puts the orientation back exactly. Custom color picker Settings › Custom color picker (on by default; untick it to get the browser’s own colour dialog back). Clicking any colour swatch — fill, stroke, side colours and their palette rows, points, the paper, the grid, the accent — opens dim’s own picker beside the panel instead of the browser’s dialog: a saturation / brightness square, a hue bar, a dropdown that switches the three number fields between HSB, HSL and RGB (your choice is remembered), and a hex field that also takes the three-digit shorthand. The number fields are the app’s own: chevrons to step, and the drag handle on their left edge to scrub a value (Shift ×10, Alt ×0.1). Enter commits a typed value, ↑ / ↓ step it by 1 (10 with Shift). Below them: Shades — two tints and five shades of the colour you picked; opened from a side colour swatch (extrude Side Color, revolve ribbon, sweep / loft colour, a palette row) on an object that has a fill, the row reads Fill shades and offers shades of that fill, so a side that belongs to its face is one click — Harmony (the complement, the two neighbouring hues, the triad), Scene (the colours this scene actually paints — fills, strokes, side colours that are switched on, drawn points, the paper — most used first, read once each time the picker opens), and Recent (the last eight colours you ended a session on). All three are rows of round swatches, eight to a line. The selected shade is marked by a thin white square just inside it. A row never rearranges itself under the click that used it. The pipette beside the before / now chip is the eyedropper. In Chrome and Edge it picks a colour from anywhere on the screen. Safari and Firefox have no such browser feature, so there it picks from inside dim: click an object on the canvas to take its paint — the stroke if you click the stroke, otherwise the fill — or the paper, or any colour swatch in the panels and in the picker itself; a small round chip beside the pointer previews what you would get. It reads the object’s own colour, not the pixel, so opacity and blend modes are not mixed in; reference images and the gizmo are skipped. Esc cancels the pick either way. The chip at the top right shows the colour before and now; click its left half to go back. Esc or a click outside closes it; clicking another swatch moves it there. It changes only the swatch it was opened from, and an undo closes it. Off = the browser’s own picker, exactly as before.
